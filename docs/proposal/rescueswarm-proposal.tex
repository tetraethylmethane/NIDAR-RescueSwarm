\begin{abstract}
India recorded 5.4 million internal displacements from disasters in 2024, the
highest figure in twelve years. After a flood, rescuers usually spend longer
finding survivors than reaching them. Boats and ground teams search slowly and
at real risk to their crews, and helicopters, though fast, are few and are
mostly needed for evacuation. This paper describes RescueSwarm, a system of
three autonomous aircraft that search an assigned area, detect and locate
survivors, and drop a standard relief kit to each one. A single operator runs
the system, and it needs no mobile network or internet connection. We present
the system architecture, the sizing and geolocation error models behind the
design, the simulation results we have so far, and a phased work plan. We buy
parts from Indian suppliers wherever a suitable one exists, mainly because
domestic lead times are about half those of imports and every week saved goes
into flight testing. Nothing has flown yet, so throughout the paper we mark
each figure as measured, modelled or assumed.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and Problem Statement}
%=============================================================================

\subsection{The problem}

India has one of the highest disaster-displacement figures in the world. The
Internal Displacement Monitoring Centre reports 5.4 million internal
displacements in India during 2024, the highest in twelve years, and 2.4 million
of them came from a single monsoon flood season in Assam~\cite{idmc2025grid}.
The scale of the problem is well documented. How quickly displaced people can be
found is much less studied.

What matters most in post-flood search and rescue is the time between the start
of an event and the moment a survivor is both found and reached. Existing
national capability is much better at the reaching than the finding. Boat teams
and ground parties get to survivors reliably once they know where to go, but
they search slowly, at considerable risk to themselves, and they cover debris
fields and rooftops poorly. Helicopters search quickly, but there are few of
them, they are expensive to fly, and they are usually busy with evacuation.

Finding people is therefore the bottleneck. An aircraft that could search an
assigned area systematically, pick out people in cluttered flood imagery and
report each one as a coordinate accurate to a few metres would let every other
rescue asset be sent where it is actually needed.

\subsection{Why several aircraft}

A single multirotor that carries a useful payload and flies for fifteen minutes
does not cover much ground. Coverage grows with the number of aircraft in the
air, but so does the workload on the people flying them, and a system that needs
one pilot per aircraft is hard to use in a disaster. The engineering problem is
therefore to fly several aircraft autonomously under one operator. That means
dividing up the area, allocating tasks, keeping the aircraft apart, combining
their reports, and behaving sensibly when the command link is lost.

\subsection{Sourcing and schedule}

We prefer Indian suppliers mainly for schedule reasons. Our supplier survey
found typical import lead times of four to six weeks, compared with two to three
weeks for domestic parts. Within an eight-week flight-test window, that
difference can decide whether we get one round of testing or two. For that
reason we name suppliers throughout and record lead time alongside price for
each part we select.

Some of our own choices work against this. To save cost, the build uses an
imported autopilot and imported propulsion instead of Indian ones, and those
parts have the longer lead times described above. We deal with this by ordering
every long-lead item in the first month, before the software work that would
normally come first. We accepted this trade of schedule margin for cost
knowingly, and the remaining risk is listed in Table~\ref{tab:risk}.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item A three-aircraft autonomous search-and-delivery system that one operator
        can run without any network, sized part by part against components
        available on the market.
  \item A geolocation error budget that treats the accuracy of survivor
        coordinates as the main output of the system, and an architecture that
        meets it using our own RTK base station rather than an external
        correction service.
  \item A coordination scheme that combines a fixed equal-area split of the
        search area, back-and-forth (boustrophedon) coverage within each part,
        and a simple greedy claim-and-lock rule for assigning survivors. We chose
        it because it is predictable and easy to verify, even though it is not
        optimal.
  \item A way of working in which every number we report can be regenerated
        from the model that produced it, and in which measured, modelled and
        assumed values are kept clearly apart.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Objectives and Scope}
%=============================================================================

\subsection{Primary objectives}

\begin{itemize}
  \item[\textbf{O1}] Search an assigned area autonomously with three aircraft
        working together, under one operator and one ground control station.
  \item[\textbf{O2}] Detect people in downward-facing imagery at a resolution
        fine enough for reliable classification, running detection on board
        and combining several frames to confirm each sighting.
  \item[\textbf{O3}] Report each detection as a map coordinate with a circular
        error probable of a few metres.
  \item[\textbf{O4}] Drop a standard relief kit at each confirmed survivor
        location.
  \item[\textbf{O5}] Work without any mobile, internet or cloud service at any
        stage of the mission.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Out of scope}

To avoid the scope being read more broadly than we intend, we list what the
system is not.

\begin{itemize}
  \item It is not for carrying people. The payload is a relief kit, and we do
        not claim it could grow into casualty evacuation.
  \item It is not certified for flight beyond visual line of sight. It flies
        inside a fixed geofence, and regulatory approval for more is future
        work.
  \item It does not work at night or in all weather. The camera sees visible
        light only. Section~\ref{sec:future} explains why we think
        near-infrared, rather than thermal, is the better extension for people
        in water.
  \item It does not triage survivors. It reports where they are and drops a
        kit, and makes no medical assessment.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work and State of the Art}
%=============================================================================

\subsection{Coverage path planning}

Dividing an area among several robots for coverage is a well-studied problem.
The DARP algorithm (Divide Areas for Optimal Multi-Robot Coverage) produces
equal-area, connected regions, one per robot, and is the usual reference
method~\cite{kapoutsis2017darp}. Within each region, the classical boustrophedon
or back-and-forth pattern~\cite{choset2000coverage} is still the standard way to
sweep a sensor with a rectangular footprint.

We use a fixed equal-area split with boustrophedon coverage inside each part
instead of a full DARP implementation. This gives up some optimality in return
for predictability: the same inputs always produce the same plan, which we can
inspect, and our verification approach (Section~\ref{sec:evidence}) depends on
that. If we later need to reallocate areas during a mission, DARP is the obvious
next step.

\subsection{Multi-robot task allocation}

The Consensus-Based Bundle Algorithm (CBBA) is the standard decentralised
auction method for assigning tasks among several agents~\cite{choi2009cbba}. Its
guarantees depend on a consensus phase over a communication network. Our system
has only three aircraft, each with a reliable link to one ground station, and
in that setting those guarantees add little. We use a simpler greedy
claim-and-lock rule with a fixed tie-break instead. It is much easier to verify,
and with three aircraft its worst case is acceptable.

\subsection{Aerial detection of people}

The main difficulty is that people seen from survey altitude are very small: an
adult lying down covers only a few tens of pixels. Slicing-aided hyper inference
(SAHI) and similar tiling methods improve recall on small objects considerably
by running the detector on overlapping crops of the full-resolution image
instead of on a shrunken whole frame~\cite{akyon2022sahi}. The standard
benchmarks show the size of the problem. COCO treats anything under
32$^2$\,px$^2$ as a small object~\cite{lin2014coco}, and TinyPerson adds finer
size bands below that because people in aerial images are often smaller still
\cite{yu2020tinyperson}.

Several aerial person datasets are available for transfer learning.
SARD~\cite{sambolek2021sard} covers search and rescue on land, and
VisDrone~\cite{zhu2021visdrone} covers urban aerial scenes. The closest match to
our case is SeaDronesSee~\cite{varga2022seadronessee}, which labels people in
open water seen from a drone. Like a flood scene, it has a low-contrast
background and targets that show only head and shoulders. None of these
datasets covers flooded Indian terrain. The proper fix would be our own field
data campaign, but that is deferred from this build (Section~\ref{sec:deferred}),
so detection recall remains a modelled figure and is the top entry in our risk
register.

\subsection{Airborne payload delivery}

In airborne delivery, most of the error comes from the aircraft's velocity and
the wind at the moment of release; the release height matters much less.
Mathisen \emph{et al.} demonstrated autonomous ballistic airdrop from a small
fixed-wing UAV with a mean delivery error of 5.5\,m. Because release conditions
dominate, they had to compute the release point continuously against wind and
aircraft velocity during flight~\cite{mathisen2020airdrop}. A multirotor can
instead slow almost to a stop before releasing, which removes the largest error
term entirely. A fixed-wing aircraft cannot do this, and it is the main reason we
chose a multirotor. Our delivery budget in Section~\ref{sec:delivery} is based
on it.

\subsection{Positioning}

Real-time kinematic (RTK) correction from a local base station is standard
practice, and it is the only way to get sub-metre relative positioning without
an external network~\cite{takasu2009rtklib}. Since our system cannot use the
internet or mobile data, it has to carry its own base station.

\subsection{Design point}

Table~\ref{tab:designpoint} gives the design point of each aircraft. All of the
values come from the sizing model described in Section~\ref{sec:sizing} and are
regenerated automatically rather than copied by hand.

One entry is not yet confirmed for the aircraft we are building. The motors we
have chosen (Section~\ref{sec:deferred}) do not come with a published thrust
curve, so the thrust-to-weight ratio is a requirement until we measure it on the
thrust stand. The other entries follow from the mission: mass, energy, altitude,
speed and payload.

The battery rows describe the pack the model was originally sized with. We have
since replaced the 18-cell Li-ion pack we planned to build ourselves with a
ready-made 6S 16\,Ah lithium-polymer pack. Section~\ref{sec:energy} gives its
figures and our reasons. We will re-run the model with the measured mass of the
new pack before first flight and update this table.

The thrust-to-weight ratio of 2.0, and a hover endurance of 15.3 minutes for a
7.6-minute mission, are safety reserves rather than performance targets. The
failure we most want to avoid is an aircraft that cannot get back. The energy
reserve is set out in Section~\ref{sec:sizing}, and it is the reserve, not the
mission itself, that determines the size of the battery.

Fig.~\ref{fig:mass} shows the mass budget behind these figures. Structure and
battery together make up 46\,\% of the take-off mass. This is why the risk
register tracks airframe mass and battery chemistry closely, while the choice of
avionics matters much less.

The items in the budget add up exactly to the maximum take-off mass, and the
model checks this. The largest optional item is the recovery parachute and its
mount at 300\,g, 4.7\,\% of the aircraft. It is the only mass carried to protect
the airframe rather than to do the mission. We have kept this allowance in the
sizing even though the parachute itself is deferred from this build
(Section~\ref{sec:deferred}). The aircraft is therefore sized to fly with the
parachute fitted, so adding it later uses up growth allowance without changing
the design point. Until then the aircraft is 300\,g lighter than the 6.36\,kg
quoted here, and every margin in this section is better by that amount. The
four relief kits weigh 800\,g in total, 12.6\,\% of take-off mass. The mission
fixes them at exactly 200\,g each, so the payload is an input to the sizing
rather than something we can trade. This is also why we cannot cut cost on
propulsion and batteries the way we can on avionics: cheaper parts of the same
size exist, but a smaller aircraft would not carry the payload.

Section~\ref{sec:sizing} works through the battery requirement in full: the
reserve that sets its size, the minimum rated energy, and the continuous current
that rules out most candidate packs. The reserve includes a second sweep because
finding nothing on the first pass is a normal result, not a failure. It includes
time to hover because three aircraft cannot all land at once.

We chose a ready-made LiPo pack over building our own Li-ion pack. We first
planned the Li-ion pack for its lower mass, longer cycle life and local
availability, while noting that LiPo was electrically better. We changed our
minds for three reasons. A suitable 6S LiPo pack is now sold by an Indian
retailer, so sourcing no longer favours Li-ion. Buying a pack means we do not
have to spot-weld eighteen cells in a student lab, which was the most hazardous
step in the build. And the electrical advantage is large: a 25\,C, 16\,Ah pack
can supply 400\,A against a peak demand of about 136\,A, whereas our own pack
would have supplied about 110\,A, and the LiPo has roughly half the voltage sag.

We could not simply swap one pack for the other, because a heavier aircraft
needs more power to hover. We ran each candidate through the sizing model at its
own mass, keeping the motors the same:

A 12 or 13\,Ah pack fails both the endurance requirement and the landing-queue
reserve, and about 14\,Ah is the smallest that passes. The 16\,Ah pack is the
smallest suitable size on sale in India. The price of the extra mass is
thrust-to-weight. The motors were sized for 2.0 at 6.36\,kg, so at 6.8--7.1\,kg
they give only 1.8--1.9, unless the P2 thrust stand shows at least 3.55\,kgf per
motor. Full thrust at that mass also raises the peak current to about
144\,A (Fig.~\ref{fig:sag}), close to the 150\,A rating of the pack fuse and
connectors, which makes the fuse the tightest electrical margin. We will weigh
the pack when it arrives and measure its voltage sag under load. The battery
failsafe uses standard LiPo values for now, and we will check them against the
measured sag before any flight.

The reserve also has to be checked at the mass the aircraft actually flies. In
the model the Li-ion pack is rounded up to a buildable 18 cells, which puts the
take-off mass about 0.7\,kg above the point where the sizing loop converges. We
therefore re-evaluate the requirement at the final design point. There it is
about a fifth higher, and the minimum rated energy rises with it. The pack still
meets the requirement at the final mass, but with a noticeably smaller margin
than a check at the converged point would show, which is why we run the check
after the loop rather than inside it.

\subsubsection{Air segment}
The air segment is three identical quadrotors. Each has a Holybro Kakute~H7
autopilot running ArduPilot~\cite{ardupilot}, mounted on a 4-in-1 speed
controller whose current sensor feeds the autopilot's battery monitor. Each also
has a dual-band RTK receiver based on the u-blox ZED-F9P, which tracks GPS L1/L2,
GLONASS G1/G2, Galileo E1/E5b and BeiDou B1/B2~\cite{ubloxzedf9p}. The receiver,
its helical antenna and a compass come as one 20\,g unit. The autopilot has no
compass of its own, so heading comes from the one in the receiver. The rest of
each aircraft is a downward-facing 12\,MP camera, a Raspberry Pi~5 with a
26\,TOPS AI accelerator, a magazine holding four kits with mechanical locks, a
5.8\,GHz digital video radio, an 865\,MHz LoRa radio, and a 2.4\,GHz ExpressLRS
link that gives the safety pilot control and an abort path independent of the
autonomy software.

\subsubsection{Ground segment}
A single ground control station gives the operator one interface for all three
aircraft. The station cannot retask an aircraft, change its waypoints or order a
drop. Abort and recall are the only commands it can send, and they are a
deliberate exception. This is built into the software rather than enforced by a
setting: the code for other commands is not present in the build we fly, so it
cannot be enabled by mistake.

\subsubsection{Positioning segment}
We use our own RTK base station. During setup it takes its position from its
first three-dimensional fix and holds that as its reference. The aircraft may
take off once they have a three-dimensional fix, but they do not report a
survivor position until they have an RTK fixed solution. Detections made before
then are tagged with the less accurate float solution and recalculated once the
fix is obtained.

Setting the base this way saves time at a cost. The base is a second ZED-F9P
with a survey-grade antenna on a tripod. A survey-in would locate it to within a
few centimetres, but it takes many minutes, and skipping it removes that time
from setup. The cost is that a base set from its first fix carries that fix's
absolute error, which is around a metre, and every survivor coordinate we report
is shifted by the same amount. RTK still gives the relative accuracy in
Table~\ref{tab:geotag}: each aircraft knows its position relative to the base to
within centimetres, wherever the base thinks it is.

For this mission we think the trade is worth it. A rescue crew will navigate to
the reported coordinate with its own receiver, which is only accurate to about
a metre anyway, so a shared offset of under a metre makes little practical
difference. Twenty minutes of survey-in, on the other hand, would come straight
out of the flying time. When there is time to set up the base before the flying
window opens, we will run the survey. We will always do so in P7 and P8, because
a measured accuracy figure has to be checked against something more accurate
than the system being measured.

The base sends corrections over its own 865\,MHz LoRa radio, and each aircraft
receives them on a matching radio. On the aircraft this radio connects to the
companion computer, because all of the autopilot's serial ports are already in
use. The companion forwards the RTCM~3.x correction stream to the autopilot as
MAVLink, and ArduPilot passes it on to the receiver using
\texttt{GPS\_INJECT\_TO}~\cite{ardupilot}. Corrections therefore travel on a
different path from both the command link and the video link. We limit the base
to MSM4 messages at 1\,Hz so that the stream fits within the radio's capacity.
If corrections are lost, the receiver falls back to SBAS, so the degraded mode
needs no extra hardware.

\subsection{Geolocation error budget}
\label{sec:geotag}

The accuracy of survivor coordinates is the main output of the system, so the
error budget is the most important analysis in the design. We take the total
error as the root sum of squares of the GNSS position error, the remaining
camera alignment (boresight) and lever-arm error, attitude error, terrain-model
error and timing error.

\subsubsection{How much the receiver matters}

One might expect positioning quality to dominate, which would make RTK essential.
The budget shows otherwise. In case~C there is effectively no GNSS position term,
because at 1\,cm it is negligible next to everything else. Two terms that no
receiver can improve, unmodelled error at 0.70\,m and the size and centre of the
target at 0.50\,m, account for 95\,\% of the variance between them
(Fig.~\ref{fig:geobudget}a).

Putting a position term back in for less accurate receivers therefore changes
the result only a little. A Monte Carlo simulation over the full budget, treating
each term as an independent two-axis Gaussian, gives:

RTK improves CEP50 by 0.16\,m over SBAS. Every receiver class meets the 5\,m
delivery requirement with more than three times margin. The simulation is

0.8326\times$RSS, and stops with an error if the two disagree. The RTK row also
reproduces, independently, the 0.75\,m requirement set by attitude error.

There is a further point. Because the base station takes its position from its
own first fix rather than a survey-in, its absolute position is off by about a
metre, and every coordinate we report carries that offset. RTK keeps the
relative accuracy in Table~\ref{tab:geotag}, with each aircraft placed to within
centimetres of the base, but against true position the difference between RTK
on an unsurveyed base and a good SBAS receiver is even smaller.

We are keeping RTK anyway, for a different reason. The budget does not fail
without it, but centimetre-level relative positioning fixes the geometry between
survivors, aircraft and landing pad precisely, and that is what we need in P7
and P8 to measure detection recall and delivery error against surveyed ground.
In other words, RTK is needed to produce reliable test results, not to meet the
mission accuracy. If funding did not stretch to it, dropping to SBAS would cost
0.16\,m of CEP50 and, more importantly, our ability to measure the system
accurately.

All three aircraft carry RTK. We had first planned to fit it to one aircraft
only, since all three are identical and one would be enough to characterise the
design. However, a ZED-F9P rover with antenna and compass now costs about the
same as the ordinary receiver plus separate compass it replaces, so fitting RTK
to every aircraft costs no more than fitting it to one. It also means any
aircraft can serve as the test aircraft, and losing one in testing does not
leave us without RTK.

\subsubsection{Required receiver accuracy}
\label{sec:receivers}

The comparison above covers three receivers. Reviewers have often asked a
broader question: why not plain GPS, or NavIC, which is Indian and costs about a
fifth as much as the RTK unit? We answer it with numbers below.

NavIC is not an alternative to RTK, because the two work at different levels.
NavIC is a satellite constellation, like GPS, GLONASS, Galileo and BeiDou,
whereas RTK is a correction technique applied to whatever constellations the
receiver tracks. The useful comparison is between classes of receiver
performance, and NavIC appears in it at its standalone accuracy, since that is
how we would use it.

Everything in the budget apart from the receiver adds up to 0.88\,m
(Fig.~\ref{fig:geobudget}a). This is a floor that no receiver can lower. Adding a
receiver error $\sigma_g$ in quadrature and converting to the 95\,\% radius gives
the condition directly:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{CEP95} = 1.7308\sqrt{\sigma_{\mathrm{rest}}^{2} + \sigma_g^{2}}
\;\le\; 5\,\text{m}
\;\Longrightarrow\;
\sigma_g \le 2.75\,\text{m}.
\label{eq:gnssbreak}
\end{equation}
So any receiver with horizontal error below 2.75\,m meets the delivery
requirement, and any receiver worse than that does not, whatever else we change.

Second, GPS alone is marginal at 4.59\,m against a 5\,m requirement: it passes on
paper but leaves nothing in hand for a day with poor satellite geometry. Third,
SBAS meets the requirement at 1.85\,m, which makes it an acceptable fallback when
RTK corrections are lost.

The SBAS figure deserves a closer look. We assumed 0.60\,m, which is optimistic;
field results for this kind of augmentation are closer to 1.0--1.5\,m. Using
Equation~(\ref{eq:gnssbreak}), the requirement is still met at either value,
with CEP95 of 2.31\,m and 3.01\,m respectively, so the conclusion does not depend
on the assumption. It would start to matter at around 3\,m, which is why we
measure the actual horizontal accuracy on the built aircraft in P5 instead of
relying on the datasheet.

Fig.~\ref{fig:geotag} shows both effects. Panel (a) shows how much positioning

alignment error or target size, so the curve leaves the ideal $1/\sqrt{n}$ line
and flattens out by about five frames. Twenty frames are not twice as good as
five, and the eight to twelve views we get anyway are already past that point.

\subsection{Perception and inference budget}
\label{sec:perception}

The camera is a 12.3\,MP module, $4056\times3040$ pixels at a 1.55\,\textmu m
pitch, behind a 6\,mm lens. We tile each frame at full resolution into 48 crops
of $640\times640$ with 20\,\% overlap, without shrinking it first. At 2\,Hz this
is 96 detector runs per second. Published measurements for this accelerator with
the same model and input size are 130--160 per second, so it fits with some
margin.

We considered halving the resolution before tiling, which would cut the number
of crops from 48 to 12. That option made sense for a slower accelerator with
about a quarter of the throughput, but it throws away half of the linear
resolution the camera provides. The number of tiles depends only on the number
of pixels, so it does not change with altitude. What changes with altitude is
the size of the target in the image, and the effect is larger than it first
appears:

we need both changes together: either one on its own leaves the target in the
size range where detectors perform worst.

mission.

takes more passes. At 40\,m the extra passes add only seconds to a mission with
minutes to spare, and the mission is actually shorter than at 60\,m because the
aircraft has less height to climb.

\subsection{Division of the computing hardware}
\label{sec:compute}

Each aircraft carries a camera, a single-board computer and an AI accelerator.
On an aircraft this small it is fair to ask why these are not one part. The
reason is that each does a job the others cannot.

The accelerator is an M.2 module that connects only over PCIe. It has no camera
input, no image processor and no general-purpose cores; it simply runs a compiled
network on data that the host computer passes to it. The camera produces a raw
Bayer stream, which has to be demosaiced, white-balanced and scaled before it is
a usable image. The tiling our detection approach depends on, cutting each frame
into 48 overlapping crops, sending them to the accelerator and mapping the
detections back onto the full frame, also has to run on the host. So does
everything else the aircraft does: the mission state machine, MAVLink routing,
the data link, claiming and releasing survivors, the delivery manoeuvre and the
telemetry sent to the ground station.

The three parts are therefore a camera, a computer that processes images and
runs the mission, and an accelerator that runs the network. Removing any one of
them would break the detection chain rather than simplify the aircraft.

There is one alternative that would remove a part, and we considered it
carefully. Some camera sensors now include an AI engine on the chip. They run the
network themselves and output detections instead of images, use about 40\,\%
less power than a separate accelerator, and would remove the accelerator
altogether. They do not suit this mission. These on-sensor engines hold a model
of only a few megabytes and run once per frame at a fixed input size. Our design
needs \textbf{48} inferences per frame, because a survivor covers only a few tens
of pixels in a 12\,MP frame and tiling is what makes such small targets
detectable (Section~\ref{sec:pipeline}). A sensor that cannot tile its own image
cannot do this, and we would still need the host computer for everything listed
above.

\subsection{Onboard processing pipeline}
\label{sec:pipeline}

Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows what runs where. The key point is that the
autopilot flies the aircraft and the companion computer does not interfere with
it during the sweep. The search lines are uploaded during setup as an ordinary
\textsc{auto} mission, and the aircraft would fly the whole search pattern even
with the companion computer switched off. The companion watches, detects, and
takes control only to make a delivery. This is a short \textsc{guided} manoeuvre,
after which the aircraft returns to the same \textsc{auto} mission.

Keeping this boundary has two benefits. If the companion crashes or overheats,
we lose detections but not control of the aircraft. And the search path can be
reproduced from the mission file alone, which lets us check the coverage and
separation results in Section~\ref{sec:evidence} directly.

\subsection{Communications}
\label{sec:comms}

The competition rules require the ground station to show a live camera feed from
each aircraft, and the system must work without any external network. We use
three radio links, each in a different band, and size each one so that it has
margin to spare at the edge of the flying area.

\textit{Video (digital, 5.8\,GHz, one channel per aircraft).} Each companion
computer draws its detections onto the camera image and sends it using
wfb-ng~\cite{wfbng} on an RTL8812EU radio. The operator and the judges therefore
see what the aircraft's software sees, rather than a raw feed. Each of the three
aircraft has its own channel. At the ground station there is one receiver per
channel, each with a 14\,dBi panel antenna pointed at its aircraft and an
omnidirectional antenna on its second port. The second port also handles the
short uplink the protocol needs, so neither port is left unconnected.

The companion computer has no hardware video encoder, so it has to encode the
video in software. We have accepted this load and limited it: each computer
encodes only its own stream, the detector runs on the accelerator rather than
the processor, and if the processor gets busy it is the video resolution and
frame rate that drop, never the number of detection tiles. In P5 we will measure
how much processor capacity is left with both running.

\textit{Mission data and coordination (SX1262, 865--867\,MHz).} This link carries
position, flight mode, battery, fix quality, survivor tags and delivery status
as one compact binary message per aircraft per second, instead of raw MAVLink.
The band is licence-free in India for short-range devices. At SF7 it has a 54\,dB
margin, the largest of any link, which suits the link that carries survivor
coordinates.

That margin also lets us choose a faster mode. Working out the channel load from
data size and bit rate alone gives the wrong answer, because for short packets
LoRa's preamble and header take most of the airtime: at SF7 the fleet's messages
fill a quarter of the channel, not the few per cent the bit rate suggests. The
same radio can run GFSK at 50\,kbps, which uses 19\,dB of the spare margin, and
that is the mode we use, with SF7 kept as a fallback at a lower message rate.
Section~\ref{sec:links} works through both, and Table~\ref{tab:coord} gives the
results.

\textit{Command and abort (ExpressLRS, 2.4\,GHz).} This is the only link that can
command the aircraft, and it connects straight to the flight controller rather
than going through the autonomy software. If the companion computer hangs or is
compromised, the safety pilot still has control. This follows from how the
hardware is wired, not from any software permission.

ExpressLRS has supported MAVLink natively since version~3.5, so the receiver that
gives the safety pilot manual control also carries the autopilot's command and
telemetry stream. On the ArduPilot side it needs only a serial port set to
MAVLink v2~\cite{elrsmavlink}. RTK corrections do not use this link; they come
from our base station on 865\,MHz (Section~\ref{sec:geotag}).

We originally planned an 802.11 mesh network, but dropped it because of its link
budget. At the edge of the geofence it had only 8.7\,dB of margin, the weakest
link in the system, and it would have carried video, telemetry and coordination
all at once. A mesh does not fix that, because a relay hop is just as weak.
Rule~8.17 forbids relying on an external network for coordination between
drones, but it does not require the aircraft to talk to each other directly, and
coordination through our own ground station counts as a locally deployed system
under rule~8.5. The cost of this arrangement is that one aircraft cannot relay
for another that has lost its link, and the link-loss failsafe already covers
that case. The benefit is that no band carries more than one job.

\subsection{Payload delivery}
\label{sec:delivery}

Kits are released 6\,m above the ground, and only once the aircraft's
remaining ground speed is below 0.3\,m/s. Under these conditions the fall itself
adds about 0.32\,m to the delivery error. Combined with geolocation and hover
error, the total is about 1.5\,m (root sum of squares) with RTK working. The
release height is low enough to keep wind drift under a metre in realistic
conditions, and high enough to stay safely clear of people and debris.

Each kit station has a mechanical lock, so a power failure cannot release a kit.
We treat this as safety-critical and will not cut cost on it.

\subsection{Mission sequence}

Fig.~\ref{fig:flow} shows the autonomous mission sequence. Three features are
worth pointing out. First, the take-off delays are written into the mission file
as a pause before each take-off command, rather than relying on the operator's
timing. This is what increases the closest approach at launch from 1.31\,m to
64.80\,m (Fig.~\ref{fig:launch}). Second, every abnormal condition ends in the
same way: link loss, low battery and operator abort all lead to return-to-launch.
Third, the battery response has two stages. At the reserve threshold the aircraft
returns to launch, and at a second, lower threshold it lands immediately where
it is. If a battery cannot get the aircraft back, it is better to land under

\subsection{Onboard health monitoring}
\label{sec:health}

Two more small models run on board, separate from survivor detection. Both work
on simple sensor signals and are small enough to run on a microcontroller, so
they take nothing from the accelerator, which Section~\ref{sec:perception} shows
is already busy.

The first detects propulsion problems. We already log current for each motor and
vibration of the airframe for the P6 tests. The same signals can reveal an
unbalanced propeller, a worn bearing or a loose mount before it causes a failure
in flight. That matters when we are flying twelve motors on three aircraft over
an eight-week campaign, and the training data comes from flight testing anyway.

The second estimates the health of the battery. The reserve in
Section~\ref{sec:sizing} uses a fixed rule: never discharge below 20\,\%. That
rule suits the chemistry but ignores cell ageing and temperature, so it is too
cautious when the pack is new and not cautious enough when it is old. A model
that uses voltage, current and temperature under load can estimate the usable
energy left directly. Since the reserve is what determines the pack size
(Section~\ref{sec:energy}), a better estimate is the cheapest way to gain margin
or flying time.

For both models, a wrong answer costs some energy or an unnecessary precautionary
landing, but never a missed survivor. We only allow small models where that is
true, which is why neither is part of survivor detection.

\subsection{Verification strategy}
\label{sec:evidence}

Our approach to verification grew out of a kind of mistake we kept making.
Four times during the preparatory work, we found a configuration file that was
correct, tested and reviewed, but was never actually used by the running system.
In one case a checked set of failsafe parameters was never loaded by any
simulation, so every test had run on the default settings, in which the
low-battery action was switched off. In another, a message-routing configuration
started without errors and then forwarded no messages at all for weeks. In every
case the file had passed review and its tests.

Our response is a working rule rather than a tool: we do not count anything as
demonstrated until the real configuration has been run end to end and the
resulting values have been read back from the running system. Every claim in
this proposal is labelled with the kind of evidence behind it:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textit{Measured}: observed on a running system, in software or in
        hardware-in-the-loop simulation.
  \item \textit{Modelled}: calculated by a model whose inputs are assumptions,
        and regenerated automatically on every change.
  \item \textit{Assumed}: neither measured nor modelled, and listed as a risk.
\end{itemize}

An automated check regenerates every model output we publish and compares it
with the committed version, so changing a model without updating its output
causes the check to fail.

\section{Preliminary Results}
%=============================================================================

We have done a good deal of work already. We summarise it below and say what
kind of evidence supports each result, so that readers can check it.

\subsection{Measured in simulation}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textit{Low-battery failsafe.} With three software-in-the-loop
        autopilots running at once, the failsafe returned each aircraft to the
        pad. Return-to-launch triggered at 10\,809\,mAh against a planned
        10\,800\,mAh.
  \item \textit{Three aircraft on one ground station.} A single telemetry router
        delivered three separate system IDs to one ground station port, and all
        three arrived.
  \item \textit{Separation at launch.} Putting take-off delays in the mission
        file, instead of relying on the operator's timing, increased the closest
        approach between airborne aircraft during launch from 1.31\,m to
        64.80\,m (Fig.~\ref{fig:launch}). Both figures are recalculated directly
        from the recorded telemetry.
  \item \textit{Separation during the sweep.} Away from the pad the aircraft
        never came closer than 29.19\,m. Different transit altitudes of 20, 25
        and 30\,m keep them apart vertically regardless of the horizontal plan.
  \item \textit{Separation at recovery.} Placing the landing slots at the
        corners of the pad instead of in a row increased slot spacing from
        1.22\,m to 2.61\,m at no cost. The measured closest approach rose from
        0.83\,m, where the aircraft overlapped, to 2.27\,m, leaving 1.22\,m
        between airframes (Fig.~\ref{fig:pad}). Separation while the aircraft
        wait above the pad improves in the same way.
  \item \textit{Ground station.} All eight required operator displays worked
        with three autopilots running at once, using offline map tiles
        (Fig.~\ref{fig:gcs}). We tested the video wall with three streams in a
        separate run with the media server switched on. It was off when the
        screenshot in Fig.~\ref{fig:gcs} was taken, which is why the video
        panels show as offline.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{No margin at the pad edge}

In Fig.~\ref{fig:pad} the corner slots sit exactly on the inner square. The
nominal clearance to the edge is 0.523\,m, one airframe radius, so a stationary
aircraft on its slot lies entirely inside the pad and meets the rule.

However, this leaves no margin at all. The rules do not allow any part of an
aircraft outside the pad during launch and landing. A touchdown error of
$\pm$0.5\,m, the figure we use elsewhere for landing accuracy, could put up to
half a metre of an airframe over the edge.

The two requirements conflict: moving the slots inward to gain edge margin
reduces the spacing between aircraft, which is the reason we moved them to the
corners in the first place. To settle this we need to measure the actual
touchdown error, which we will do in P6. We mention it here because the layout
looks safe in the figure but is only safe on average.

\subsection{Modelled}

Endurance, hover power, mass budget, thermal margin, link budget, delivery error
and geolocation error are all modelled, and all regenerate from source. The
geolocation Monte Carlo agrees with a separate analytic calculation to within one
per cent. We have also checked the code that converts a detection into a ground
position against a second, independent formulation, and the two agree to
$7.8\times10^{-10}$\,m.

\subsection{Obstacles}
\label{sec:obstacles}

The aircraft fly their sweeps without commands from the ground, so avoiding
buildings has to be built into the design. Three measures do this, and there is
one thing the aircraft cannot do.

Flying altitude clears low buildings only. The sweep is flown at 40\,m above
ground, which is well above the two- to four-storey buildings typical of rural
and semi-urban flood plains. It is not high enough for cities. Chennai and
Mumbai, where serious flooding happens, have many buildings taller than 40\,m.

The aircraft also cannot simply fly higher. The survey altitude is set by
detection: at 40\,m a person in water covers 1498\,px\textsuperscript{2}, above
the COCO small-object threshold, but at 50\,m the same person covers only
959\,px\textsuperscript{2} and falls below it. No altitude both clears a 60\,m
building and lets this camera resolve a 0.4\,m target. Any obstacle taller than
the sweep height therefore has to be avoided by going around it.

Avoiding obstacles sideways relies on marking them in advance. The operator
draws the search area on site, and ArduPilot's exclusion zones let a pylon, a
tower or a tall building be marked as an area the aircraft will not enter. The
autopilot enforces these zones itself, so they still apply even if the mission
plan is wrong. But they only work if someone has seen the obstacle and drawn it.

Height above the surface is measured directly. A downward-facing lidar gives
the true height above whatever is below the aircraft. This matters most at
release. The kit is meant to drop from 6\,m, but a barometer measures height
above the launch point, which over floodwater of unknown and changing depth is
not the same thing. Section~\ref{sec:delivery} shows that impact speed and
landing error both grow with release height, so a kit dropped from what the
aircraft thinks is 6\,m but is really 15\,m behaves quite differently from our
analysis. The lidar reaches about 7\,m outdoors, so it picks up the surface
during the descent and controls the release. It does not look forward and is
not an obstacle sensor.

The aircraft cannot detect obstacles ahead of it. Nothing on board can see a
structure in its path, so a tall building that has not been marked as an
exclusion zone is invisible to the system, and the aircraft would fly into it.

This limits where the system can be used. As designed, it is for open water and
low-rise flooding, where 40\,m is above everything and an exclusion zone can
cover the occasional pylon. It is not suitable for flooded cities with tall
buildings. Adding a forward sensor would not be enough on its own: the aircraft
could only stop or turn, not climb, so operating among tall buildings would need
a route planner that finds a way around obstacles at a fixed altitude, which is
a separate project. A 360-degree lidar costs around INR 10,000 and weighs 190\,g,
well within the 4.9\,kg growth allowance, and could be useful as a last-resort
stop. However, an unfunded and untested sensor flown for the first time at the
competition would add more risk than it removes. If we buy one, we will test it
in P6.

\subsection{Assumed or not yet implemented}
\label{sec:notimpl}

Several figures are still assumptions: the remaining camera alignment error
after calibration, the RTK accuracy we will actually achieve in the field, and
the battery's internal resistance in Indian summer temperatures. Each will be
measured in P5--P8. Detection recall on real flood imagery is also an assumption,
and because the field campaign is deferred (Section~\ref{sec:deferred}) we do not
currently have a plan to measure it. That is why it is at the top of the risk
register.

One ground station function is not implemented, and Fig.~\ref{fig:gcs} shows
this. The abort and recall buttons record the operator's command in the mission
log but send nothing, because the command link from the ground is not yet wired.
For now, recovery depends on the safety pilot's transmitter. We will close this
in P4 using the 2.4\,GHz ExpressLRS link, since the separate sub-gigahertz radio
is deferred. That makes the ExpressLRS link essential for safety, not just a
convenience for manual control.

\subsection{Evidence status}

Nothing in this project has flown yet. Every result we describe as measured was
measured in simulation or in software. That rules out many logic and integration
errors, but none of the physical ones. The main purpose of the build is to turn
the modelled and assumed figures in this section into measured ones.

\section{Scope of This Build}
\label{sec:deferred}

This document describes the technical system. The funding request is a separate
document. This section covers what the build includes and leaves out, because
several arguments earlier in the paper depend on it.

\subsection{Deferred items}

We have deferred the following items, not dropped them as unnecessary. We list
them so that reviewers can see what the lowest-cost version gives up, and
because two of them limit what this paper can claim.

The field data campaign and the ground-truth equipment are both deferred. As a
result, detection recall in this paper is modelled, not measured, and we
currently have no funded way to measure it. Of everything in this list, this has
the biggest effect on what the system can be shown to do, and it is the first
item we would restore if funding allowed.

The second GNSS receiver on each aircraft is deferred. It would provide
moving-baseline heading, but Section~\ref{sec:geotag} shows that using
compass-based attitude instead costs only 18\,mm in the case-C budget. The real
reason to want it is protection against the compass being disturbed by
high-current wiring nearby, which the budget does not model.

Our receiver does make this possible later: two ZED-F9P receivers on one
aircraft, one in moving-base mode, provide heading, and ArduPilot supports this
setup. We have deferred it for mass and cost, not availability, so heading
currently comes from the compass in the rover unit. In P6 we will measure the
heading accuracy on the built aircraft, and if it is worse than about
1$^\circ$ we will fit the second receiver.

The separate sub-gigahertz safety radio is deferred. This depends on the main
link being configured correctly: it must run in the mode that carries both
manual control and MAVLink telemetry over one link and one autopilot serial
port. In the alternative transparent-serial mode the link carries only
telemetry, and the aircraft has no manual control. We will confirm the correct
mode on the bench in P2; if it does not work, the safety radio is no longer
optional.

Recovery parachutes are allowed by the rules but not required. A crash costs
50 points against 10 for landing outside the zone, so deferring parachutes means
risking about forty points on the chance of a crash.

Spares are limited to parts that break in crashes. We keep spare propellers,
motors, arms, plate stock and battery packs, but no spare autopilot, GNSS
receiver or camera. We accept the risk that a failure of one of these during the
flight-test window would cost us time, and our only protection is ordering
replacements quickly.

Flight insurance is deferred, but whether it is required must be checked against
the rulebook and the Drone Rules before any flight. Unlike the other items, this
is not just a performance trade-off.

\subsection{A limitation of our ground truth}

We will make the ground-truth targets ourselves rather than buy them. The
survivor targets are clothed forms of the right size and shape; a detector
responds to outline, scale and contrast against the background, not to anatomy.
For two items, making our own is actually better. We can make dummy kits weigh
exactly the 200\,g that rule C6 requires, and donated clothing gives a wider
range of colours and contrasts than matching bought sets, which helps test how
robust detection is.

We will survey the calibration markers with our own RTK rover. This is normal
practice and good enough for the purpose, but it means the reference positions
we measure geolocation against are themselves RTK-derived. The errors in the two
are therefore correlated, and any geolocation accuracy we report from P7 and P8
will be slightly better than a comparison against an independently surveyed
reference would show. We will say so whenever we report those figures.

\section{Security and Data Protection}
\label{sec:security}
%=============================================================================

Three features of the design also serve security, and we built them in from the
start.

The ground station cannot command the aircraft. In the build we fly, the server
has no command routes at all, so the operator interface cannot send a waypoint,
a mode change or a release. This is stronger than access control, because there
is nothing to bypass.

Command authority is kept apart from the autonomy software. ExpressLRS connects
straight to the flight controller, not through the companion computer. If that
computer hangs, is compromised or runs a model that misbehaves, the safety pilot
still has control. This follows from how the hardware is connected.

Nothing depends on an outside network. All three links (Section~\ref{sec:comms})
use licence-free bands with no infrastructure between the aircraft and the
ground station, so there is no route for a remote attack.

\subsection{Link security}

The mission data link carries survivor coordinates, so it is encrypted. The video
link uses wfb-ng's key-pair encryption, and ExpressLRS uses a binding phrase that
is kept out of the code repository and is different for each aircraft. Neither
adds noticeable delay. An unencrypted link would also be harder to defend under
the rules, since rule~8.4 prohibits public networks and an open link looks more
like one.

In a disaster area the realistic threat to positioning is interference rather
than deliberate spoofing: crowded spectrum, other responders' equipment, and
signals reflecting off water and buildings. Tracking four constellations on two
frequency bands gives some protection if one is degraded, and the operator checks
fix quality before launch. We have not yet evaluated integrity monitoring on the
receiver.

\subsection{Survivor data as personal data}

The system records the precise locations of identifiable people in distress,
along with aerial images of them. Under the Digital Personal Data Protection Act
2023 this is personal data, and the people concerned are in no position to give
or refuse consent when it is collected.

We therefore follow four rules about what happens to this data afterwards:

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
  \item Coordinates are sent to the operator and kept only for the duration of
        the operation. They are deleted after handover to the rescue authority.
  \item Images are kept only when they are needed as evidence for a specific
        measurement, and they stay on the aircraft rather than being
        transmitted.
  \item We will not publish any image of an identifiable person, in this paper,
        in our results or in any dataset, unless the face and body are
        permanently obscured.
  \item We train detection models on public datasets whose subjects agreed to
        that use, not on images of real casualties.
\end{enumerate}

The fourth rule has a cost. It is part of the reason detection recall remains
modelled rather than measured, and why our perception testing
(Section~\ref{sec:evidence}) uses home-made targets and public sea imagery
instead of real flood imagery, which would be more representative. We accept
lower-quality measurement rather than collect data we should not hold.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}
%=============================================================================

This proposal covers one prototype build. The work could go further, and we
outline the next steps here to make the limits of the present scope clear.

\subsection{From prototype to usable system}

This build is not a product. Getting there would need, roughly in this order:
night operation, discussed below; approval to fly beyond visual line of sight,
which is mainly a regulatory task rather than an engineering one; protection
against water, dust and heat for monsoon conditions; and longer endurance than
the present 15.3 minutes, which at this payload means a different type of
airframe rather than a better battery.

For night operation, the obvious choice is not the best one. Thermal imaging is
the usual answer and works well over rooftops, dry debris and open ground. It
works poorly, though, for exactly the case this system is designed for. A person
in floodwater cools towards the water's temperature, so the contrast a thermal
camera relies on largely disappears, and what remains comes only from the head
and shoulders, the smallest part of an already small target.

Near-infrared suits this case better. Water absorbs strongly above about
900\,nm while skin reflects comparatively well, so a person appears bright
against dark water. The same effect is why near-infrared is widely used to map
water in remote sensing. It is also about ten times cheaper than a thermal
camera, since it only needs a camera with its infrared filter removed, and it
can be evaluated on public imagery before buying anything. The two would
complement each other: thermal for survivors on dry ground at night, and
near-infrared for survivors in water.

\subsection{How little computing is enough?}

Section~\ref{sec:perception} shows that we cannot meet this task by shrinking the
image, because for a fixed camera the size of a person in pixels and the area
covered per pass trade against each other. A smaller detector input needs either
a lower altitude or a longer lens, and both narrow the swath. What that argument
does not tell us is where the limit actually lies.

Finding that limit would be useful in its own right. We could run the same tiled
detection, on the same images, on three levels of hardware: the 26~TOPS
accelerator used here, its 13~TOPS version, and a microcontroller-class device.
For each we would record the altitude, tile count and detection rate at which
recall drops below the requirement. The result would be a curve of achievable
coverage rate against computing power. As far as we know, this has not been
published for aerial person detection, and it is what a designer would need.

One application is flight on other planets, where power is severely limited.
\emph{Ingenuity} made 72 flights on Mars with a 1.8\,kg helicopter in an
atmosphere less than one per cent as dense as Earth's, and \emph{Dragonfly} is
due to launch for Titan in 2028. In both cases commands take minutes to arrive,
so all perception decisions have to be made on board. Microcontroller-class
detection is often proposed for such missions, and our measurement would show
what target size that choice gives up.

\subsection{Cost in larger numbers}

Our part prices are single-unit retail prices for a build of three. Each extra
aircraft would already cost noticeably less than our per-aircraft figure,
because the ground station, test equipment and spares are bought only once. At
production volumes the important factors would be different, such as tooling,
negotiated component prices and assembly labour, and we cannot estimate these
reliably from a three-aircraft bill of materials, so we do not try.

\subsection{Possible commercial use}

The likely customers are state disaster management authorities and the national
and state disaster response forces. To judge whether this could be a viable
business, we would need three things this project does not attempt: how many
such systems these agencies would buy and how often, what they currently pay
for similar capability, and the hourly operating cost of the boat or helicopter
searches it would replace. These questions need published tender data and
interviews with operators. We leave them open rather than guess.

One useful by-product of this project is a part-by-part record of which UAV
components we could source in India and which we could not. That record will be
useful whatever happens after this build.

%=============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
%=============================================================================

We have described a system of three autonomous aircraft intended to speed up
the slowest part of post-flood rescue, which is finding survivors. Throughout
the work we have tried to keep what has been demonstrated separate from what has
only been designed. The items left out of this build were deferred to save cost,
and Section~\ref{sec:deferred} sets out what each one costs the system.

The design point and error budgets are complete and can be reproduced.
Coordination and failsafe behaviour have been shown in simulation with three
autopilots running at once. What remains is to build and fly the aircraft and
replace the modelled and assumed figures in Section~\ref{sec:evidence} with
measured ones.

Our claim for the work so far is a modest one: every figure in this paper can be
checked against the model that produced it, and every figure that has not yet
been measured is marked as such.
