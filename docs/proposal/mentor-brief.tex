\section{Executive summary}

Floods displace millions of people in India every year. The slow part of a
rescue is not reaching survivors --- it is \emph{finding} them.

We are building three one-metre, 6.4\,kg multirotor aircraft that search flooded
ground as a coordinated group under \textbf{one operator}, recognise people from
the air, report their position to about a metre, and drop a relief kit to each.
It needs \textbf{no mobile network and no internet} --- among the first things a
flood removes.

The sizing, error budgets and flight software are written down and
reproducible, and coordination has been demonstrated across three simulated
autopilots. \textbf{No aircraft has flown yet}, so every number here is
calculated rather than measured, and the plan below is arranged to replace them
with measurements in order of how much each one matters.

We are asking for \textbf{\rs{7{,}34{,}379} across 32 steps}, none above
\rs{30{,}000}, each released only on the result of the one before. The first is
\rs{26{,}196}.

%-----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{The problem}

\textbf{After a flood, the binding constraint is time-to-locate.} Boat teams
search slowly and at high risk and cannot see onto rooftops. Helicopters are
scarce, costly per hour, and committed to evacuation. Nothing fills the gap
between them: covering a large area quickly and reporting exactly where people
are.

Small drones look like the answer and mostly are not: they need one pilot each,
depend on a network that the disaster has removed, find people without helping
them --- and seeing a person from the air is genuinely hard.

\textbf{That last part is the hard one.} A person in floodwater shows head
and shoulders only, about 40\,cm across. A detector takes a fixed, small input,
so the usual approach shrinks the image to fit --- and the person shrinks with
it. Published results at the resulting size report only 29--61\,\% accuracy, and
choosing a smaller, faster model makes it \emph{worse}, because smaller models
take smaller inputs.

Two further things make floods harder still: no flood dataset labels
\emph{people}, and thermal cameras fail in water, where skin cools to water
temperature and the heat difference disappears.

%-----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Our solution}

\textbf{The system.} The operator draws a search area of any shape; the software
divides it into three balanced pieces and plans a path over each --- verified on
thirteen awkward shapes with no path leaving the region and under 0.02\,\%
imbalance. The aircraft then fly themselves: our harness proves structurally
that no command is sent after the mission starts. Detection and geolocation run
\emph{on board}, so nothing depends on a radio link. Each aircraft carries four
kits and can descend, stop over a survivor and release one. Link loss, low
battery and operator abort all converge on the same return-and-land behaviour.

\textbf{How we handle it.} We do not shrink the image. The full-resolution
frame is cut into 48 overlapping tiles and each is examined properly --- standard
practice in the field --- and we fly at 40\,m rather than 60\,m. Together those
take the person from 13 pixels to \textbf{39}, which is nine times the area and
moves them out of the size band where detectors perform worst.

That costs four times the computing, and we checked it fits before adopting it:
48 tiles at 2\,Hz is 96 inferences per second, against 130--160 measured for the
accelerator we are buying. The extra transects at the lower altitude cost
seconds, and the mission is in fact slightly \emph{shorter} than at 60\,m
because the climb is lower.

\textbf{One item remains open.} Confirming a survivor across twelve camera
frames would need about 3\,Hz rather than 2\,Hz, which does \emph{not} fit. We
have a two-stage approach that would buy it back --- a cheap model skipping the
roughly 87\,\% of tiles that are empty water --- and we intend to test it on free
public data before adopting it, because a cheap first look that discards a tile
containing a person fails silently.

%-----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Deliverables}

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
\item Three aircraft flying as a coordinated group under one operator.
\item A ground station that shows everything and is deliberately \emph{unable}
to fly the aircraft, so the autonomy cannot be quietly undermined.
\item Autonomy software --- area division, path planning, allocation, failsafes
--- with an automated test suite.
\item A \emph{measured} detection figure, replacing the calculated one. The
first real number we would have.
\item A measured answer on whether a cheap first-pass filter can raise the
capture rate enough to confirm survivors across twelve frames.
\item A possible publication: tiling, aerial person detection and low-power
onboard inference have not been combined for flood conditions.
\item Code and results published either way. A negative result settles the same
question and costs the same.
\end{enumerate}

%-----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Benefits}

\textbf{Operational.} Three aircraft sweep ten hectares in under eight minutes
and hand a coordinator a list of coordinates rather than a video feed somebody
must watch --- with no network, which is the condition that actually obtains.

\textbf{Economic.} The whole system costs less than one hour of helicopter
search time, travels by road, and is run by two people.

\textbf{Departmental.} What outlives the project: a calibrated thrust stand, a
certified scale, a ground station, a tested autonomy codebase, and students who
have taken a system from arithmetic to flight.

\textbf{Strategic.} Indian suppliers wherever they meet the specification --- for
sovereignty, and because domestic lead times are half those of imports, which
buys flight-test days.

\textbf{Transferable.} Nothing is flood-specific except the training data. The
same system suits earthquake rubble survey, wildfire perimeter mapping and
avalanche search.

Steps 17--32 repeat steps 3--10 exactly, once per aircraft, none above
\rs{29{,}440}. By the time any is requested a complete aircraft has already
flown --- so what is funded is \textbf{replication of a working design}.
(Individual steps are rounded to the rupee, so the column sums to within a few
rupees of the total.)

\textbf{Named parts.} Holybro Pixhawk 6C~Mini; Raspberry~Pi~5 8\,GB with the
Pi AI HAT+ (Hailo-8); Arducam IMX477 with a 6\,mm \emph{fixed-focus} lens
(hyperfocal distance is 4.15\,m, so focus set once is correct at every altitude
we fly); Molicel P45B cells, 4500\,mAh at 45\,A because peak draw is 38.3\,A per
cell; Tarot TL96020 motors; ToolkitRC~M6D chargers; RadioMaster Boxer.

\textbf{Two notes.} The obvious charger, the HOTA D6~Pro at
\rs{10{,}499}--\rs{13{,}500}, is 325\,W per channel and can barely charge our
packs; the ToolkitRC M6D is 500\,W at \rs{5{,}836} and needs only a power supply
the department already owns. And one line is \emph{under}-budgeted: propellers
are carried at \rs{1{,}000} each against a verified \rs{3{,}250} per pair ---
about \rs{7{,}500} short across three aircraft, on the item most often broken.
We will seek a cheaper supplier and absorb the rest from contingency.

\textbf{What this costs.} Thirty-two approvals is real administrative work, and
buying one aircraft at a time means placing the same imported order three times
--- roughly eight to twelve weeks of schedule. We have chosen it anyway, because
we would rather move slowly with your confidence than quickly without it. If
either becomes binding, merging the per-aircraft steps is the lever, and that is
your call.

\textbf{For the detection test we need nothing new}: about a week on a
departmental GPU and 30\,GB of storage. The dataset is public, the software open
source.

\section{Future work}

\textbf{Near term.} Replace every modelled number with a measured one ---
detection accuracy, motor thrust, setup time. Test near-infrared sensing: water
absorbs infrared and people do not, so a person appears bright against dark
water. A camera without its infrared filter costs about \rs{3{,}000} and a
public dataset exists to test it before buying. Beyond floods, the same system
suits earthquake, wildfire and avalanche search.

\textbf{Longer term: aircraft that must think for themselves.} Our central
constraint --- a capable aircraft deciding for itself inside a very small power
budget --- is a hard requirement in planetary aviation. NASA's \emph{Ingenuity}
flew 72 times on Mars from a 1.8\,kg airframe in an atmosphere under one per
cent of Earth's density. \emph{Dragonfly} launches in 2028 for Titan, where the
air is four times denser than Earth's at one seventh the gravity.

What makes those hard is not the flying but that \textbf{a radio command takes
minutes to arrive}: nobody is piloting, so every choice is made on board. That
is our problem with the difficulty raised, and what transfers is what is not
about floods --- planning a survey on board, running perception to an
\emph{energy} budget, and failing safely with no link.

\textbf{We should not overstate it.} Our own analysis found that
microcontroller-class processors --- the hardware usually proposed for such
missions --- cannot see our targets at all, because shrinking the image to fit
them destroys what we are looking for. A planetary version needs a different
operating point. That is a research question, not a slide.

\section{Conclusion}

The design is further along than it looks, and explicit about which numbers are
measured and which are not. We are asking for the means to finish it in 32 small
steps, none above \rs{30{,}000}, so nothing is committed before the previous
step has produced evidence.

The first step is \rs{26{,}196} and answers the question everything depends on:
can these motors lift this aircraft. The immediate technical need is smaller
still --- a week of GPU time to test the detection idea on free public data.
Having already found one sizing mistake by checking, testing first seems clearly
the better choice.

\vspace{2mm}
We would value your guidance on four decisions that are ours but that we would
rather not make alone: the body size we design against, the operating altitude,
whether to adopt the two-stage detector, and which of two processing modules to
buy.
