\section{Executive summary}

Floods displace millions of people in India every year, and the slow part of a
rescue is not reaching survivors --- it is \emph{finding} them.

We are building three one-metre, 6.4\,kg multirotor aircraft that search flooded
ground as a coordinated group under \textbf{one operator}, recognise people from
the air, report their position to about a metre, and drop a relief kit to each.
It requires \textbf{no mobile network and no internet}, which matters because
those are among the first things a flood removes.

The sizing, error budgets and flight software are complete and reproducible, and
coordination has been demonstrated across three simulated autopilots. No
aircraft has flown yet, so the numbers here are calculated; the plan below is
ordered to replace them with measurements, hardest question first.

We are asking for \textbf{\rs{7{,}43{,}004} across 32 steps}, none above
\rs{30{,}000}, each released only on the result of the one before. The first is
\rs{26{,}196}.

%-----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{The problem}

\textbf{After a flood the binding constraint is time-to-locate.} Boat teams
search slowly, at high risk, and cannot see onto rooftops. Helicopters are
scarce, costly per hour, and committed to evacuation. Nothing covers a large
area quickly \emph{and} reports exactly where people are.

Small drones look like the answer and mostly are not: they need one pilot each,
depend on a network the disaster has removed, and find people without helping
them.

The harder problem is seeing a person at all. Someone in floodwater shows head
and shoulders only, about 40\,cm across. Detectors take a fixed, small input, so
the conventional approach shrinks the image to fit --- and the person shrinks
with it, to a size where published results report only 29--61\,\% accuracy.
Choosing a smaller, faster model makes this \emph{worse}, because smaller models
take smaller inputs. Two further constraints are specific to floods: no existing
dataset labels \emph{people} in flood imagery, and thermal cameras fail in
water, where skin cools to water temperature.

%-----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Our solution}

\textbf{The system.} The operator draws a search area of any shape; the software
divides it into three balanced pieces and plans a path over each --- verified on
thirteen awkward boundary shapes with no path leaving the region and under
0.02\,\% workload imbalance. The aircraft then fly themselves, and our test
harness proves structurally that no command is sent after the mission begins.
Detection and geolocation run \emph{on board}, so nothing depends on a link.
Each aircraft carries four kits and can descend, stop over a survivor and
release one. Link loss, low battery and operator abort all converge on the same
return-and-land behaviour.

\textbf{Seeing the survivor.} We do not shrink the image. The full-resolution
frame is cut into 48 overlapping tiles, each examined at native resolution, and
we fly at 40\,m rather than 60\,m. Together these put the person at
\textbf{39 pixels} instead of 13 --- nine times the area, and out of the size
band where detectors perform worst. Only both changes together achieve it;
either alone leaves the target in the difficult band.

It costs four times the computing, and it fits: 48 tiles at 2\,Hz is 96
inferences per second against the 130--160 measured for our accelerator. The
extra transects cost seconds, and the mission is in fact slightly shorter than
at 60\,m because the climb is lower.

\textbf{One item is open.} Confirming a survivor across twelve frames needs
about 3\,Hz, which does not fit. A two-stage approach would buy it back --- a
cheap model skipping the roughly 87\,\% of tiles that are empty water --- and we
will test it on free public data before adopting it, because a first-pass filter
that discards a tile containing a person fails silently.

%-----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Deliverables}

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
\item Three aircraft flying as a coordinated group under one operator.
\item A ground station that displays everything and is deliberately \emph{unable}
to fly the aircraft, so the autonomy cannot be undermined in use.
\item Autonomy software --- area division, path planning, allocation, failsafes
--- with an automated test suite.
\item A \emph{measured} detection figure, replacing the calculated one.
\item A measured answer on whether the two-stage filter can raise the capture
rate enough to confirm survivors across twelve frames.
\item A publication: image tiling, aerial person detection and low-power onboard
inference have not been combined for flood conditions.
\item Code and results published either way.
\end{enumerate}

%-----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Benefits}

\textbf{Operational.} Three aircraft sweep ten hectares in under eight minutes
and hand a coordinator a list of coordinates rather than a video feed somebody
must watch --- with no network, which is the condition that actually obtains.

\textbf{Economic.} The whole system costs less than one hour of helicopter
search time, travels by road, and is run by two people.

\textbf{Departmental.} A calibrated thrust stand, a certified scale, a ground
station, a tested autonomy codebase and trained students all outlive the
project.

\textbf{Strategic.} Indian suppliers wherever they meet the specification --- for
sovereignty, and because domestic lead times are half those of imports, which
buys flight-test days.

\textbf{Transferable.} Nothing is flood-specific except the training data. The
same system suits earthquake rubble survey, wildfire mapping and avalanche
search.

departmental GPU and 30\,GB of storage. The dataset is public and the software
is open source.

\section{Future work}

\textbf{Near term.} Replace every calculated number with a measured one:
detection accuracy, motor thrust, setup time. Test near-infrared sensing --- water
absorbs infrared and people do not, so a person appears bright against dark
water. A camera without its infrared filter costs about \rs{3{,}000} and a public
dataset exists to test the idea before buying. Beyond floods, the same system
suits earthquake, wildfire and avalanche search.

\textbf{Longer term: aircraft that must think for themselves.} Our central
constraint --- a capable aircraft deciding for itself inside a very small power
budget --- is a hard requirement in planetary aviation. NASA's \emph{Ingenuity}
flew 72 times on Mars from a 1.8\,kg airframe in an atmosphere under one per cent
of Earth's density. \emph{Dragonfly} launches in 2028 for Titan, where the air is
four times denser than Earth's at one seventh the gravity.

What makes those hard is not the flying but that \textbf{a radio command takes
minutes to arrive}: nobody is piloting, so every choice is made on board. What
transfers from this work is what is not about floods --- planning a survey on
board, running perception to an \emph{energy} budget, and failing safely with no
link. One caution worth stating: microcontroller-class processors, the hardware
usually proposed for such missions, cannot resolve targets of the size we care
about, so a planetary version needs a different operating point. That is a
research question in its own right.

\section{Conclusion}

The design is complete and explicit about which numbers are measured and which
are calculated. We are asking for the means to finish it in 32 steps, none above
\rs{30{,}000}, so nothing is committed before the previous step has produced
evidence.

The first step is \rs{31{,}371} and answers the question everything else depends
on: can these motors lift this aircraft. The immediate technical need is smaller
still --- a week of GPU time to test the detection approach on free public data.

\vspace{2mm}
We would value your guidance on two decisions: the operating altitude, which the
detection test will inform, and whether to adopt the two-stage detector once we
have measured it.
